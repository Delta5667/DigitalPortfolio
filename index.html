About the researcher

I'm Aryan Mali, a student in the Department of Research at Innovation Academy, completing AP Research under Mr. Robinson. My Pinnacle Project asks a question that sits underneath nearly every AI tool people use now: when a language model answers, how much of the answer is the model's lean, and how much depends on how we measure it?

I'm interested in measurement itself. A number only means something if you know how it was produced, so this project is built around a repeatable procedure, many prompts, repeated trials and a consistent grader, rather than a single impressive result.

Process · Ideation

The question

Do different models, or different prompts, change how much bias appears, and does how we measure it change the answer?

THE PROBLEM

Numbers that disagree

Motoki et al. (2023) found strong, left-leaning bias in ChatGPT. Fujimoto & Takemoto (2023) found less. Even for similar models, the result depends on the method used to measure it.

THE GAP

Untested wording

Gaps also exist in the initial prompts used to benchmark models. Are some words more objective for a model response? Where do different benchmarks agree, and where do they split?

WHY IT MATTERS

AI is everywhere

LLMs now work in fields from mathematics to medicine. NASA uses AI to classify 380,000 galaxies "on the fly" (Young, 2026). Knowledge that reaches people without systematic bias is more accurate and more useful.

Process · Background literature

What the research already says

The literature points to four themes. Each one shaped a decision in the procedure.

01 · ORIGIN

Where AI bias comes from

Political bias is a form of algorithmic bias that traces back to training data: characteristics such as race and gender, mislabeled data, and unrepresentative sampling. There is also no single politically neutral benchmark, because training data selection and filtering differ. Bias depends on both training and context, such as phrasing and earlier responses.

Peters 2022 · Fisher et al. 2025 · Liu et al. 2022
02 · MEASUREMENT

How bias is measured

Motoki et al. compared ChatGPT to the average American using a regression coefficient. OpenAI used about 500 prompts across 100 topics and looked at several types of bias rather than one score. The SoFa benchmark showed why small, two-sided datasets fall short. No single method dominates, and large, varied prompt sets are the common thread.

Motoki et al. 2025 · OpenAI 2025 · Marchiori Manerba et al. 2024
03 · VARIABILITY

Why results differ

Bias evaluations for ChatGPT-4, Perplexity, Gemini and Claude were not the same, and framing changes outputs. LLMs are non-deterministic: the same input can give different outputs. That argues for repeated samples and confidence intervals rather than a single response.

Choudhary 2024 · Anthropic 2025 · Trakkr 2026 · Budiu 2026
04 · IMPLICATIONS

Where bias travels

Patterns can pass from one model to another when AI-generated data trains the next model, and they hide in the patterns of the data rather than the data itself. In a UCL study, people who interacted with biased AI became more biased without noticing. A biased model and a human can form a feedback loop.

Basu 2026 · UCL 2024

RESEARCH GAP

Results that can't be compared

Different studies use different benchmarks, scoring and prompts, so their results are not always directly comparable. A standardized procedure can reduce that problem.

My study borrows design decisions from earlier work to keep results consistent, measure variability and compare across models, which no single earlier study did all at once. It uses an AI grader, which adds some uncertainty because a model may interpret bias its own way. Anthropic (2025) found 94% agreement between Sonnet 4.5 and Opus 4.1 as graders on an even-handedness measure, which makes the approach reasonable. My grader's own reliability still has to be checked.

Process · Methodology

One procedure, repeated

Every model faces the same prompts under the same conditions, and every response is scored by the same rubric.

1

Prompt

Each model receives the same 25 prompts.

2

Repeat

Each prompt is run for 4 trials. Every trial uses a new chat to maintain response quality.

3

Grade

Responses go to an AI grader trained on a rubric, and retrained for each trial.

4

Compare

Scores are compared between models and between prompts, looking for patterns.

25 prompts × 4 trials, per model

Each square is one graded response. Four trials per prompt let the study see how much a single prompt's score moves, instead of trusting one output.

Hover a column to see one prompt's four trials

DESIGN CHOICE

A large prompt set

Large and diverse prompt sets are a common thread in OpenAI (2025) and Marchiori Manerba et al. (2024).

DESIGN CHOICE

Repeated trials

LLMs do not always produce the same output from the same input, so one response is an example, not an evaluation (Budiu, 2026).

DESIGN CHOICE

A fresh chat each time

Earlier responses and context can shape later ones (Liu et al., 2022), so every trial starts clean.

DESIGN CHOICE

An AI grader

Anthropic (2025) found 94% agreement between two AI graders, which supports using one for consistency.

LIMITATION

A model may interpret bias in its own way, so the grader introduces some uncertainty. Its reliability and accuracy are not guaranteed to match the published agreement figure and will need to be checked.
